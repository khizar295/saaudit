What is an Audit? A Complete Guide for NZ Organisations

8 min read Published 2025 Sheed Ali, CA ANZ Qualified Auditor

If you've been told your organisation needs an audit, you might be wondering what exactly that means. Will it be disruptive? How long will it take? What will the auditor actually do? This guide answers these questions and more — helping you understand the audit process from start to finish.

What is a Financial Audit?

A financial audit is an independent examination of an organisation's financial statements conducted by a qualified auditor. The purpose is to determine whether the financial statements present a true and fair view of the organisation's financial position and performance, in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework.

In New Zealand, financial audits are conducted in accordance with International Standards on Auditing (New Zealand) (ISAs (NZ)), which are issued by the External Reporting Board (XRB). The auditor issues an audit report expressing their opinion on the financial statements.

Why Do Organisations Need an Audit?

There are several reasons an organisation might need or choose to have an audit:

  • Statutory requirements — Many organisations are required by law to have their financial statements audited, including large companies, registered charities above certain thresholds, and incorporated societies.
  • Lender requirements — Banks and financial institutions often require audited financial statements as a condition of lending.
  • Funder requirements — Grant providers and funding bodies may require audited financials to demonstrate accountable use of funds.
  • Stakeholder confidence — An independent audit provides shareholders, members, donors, and other stakeholders with confidence in the reliability of financial information.
  • Good governance — Many organisations choose voluntary audits as a matter of good practice and transparency.

Types of Audit Engagements

Not all audit engagements are the same. The main types include:

1. Financial Statement Audit

The most common type — an independent examination of an organisation's complete financial statements to provide a high level of assurance. The auditor issues an opinion on whether the financial statements give a true and fair view.

2. Review Engagement

A review provides moderate assurance — less than an audit but more than nothing. The auditor performs analytical procedures and makes enquiries, rather than detailed testing. Suitable where a full audit isn't required.

3. Agreed-Upon Procedures

The auditor performs specific procedures agreed upon with the engaging party and reports factual findings. No assurance opinion is provided. This is the most targeted and often most cost-effective option.

4. Special Purpose Audit

An audit of financial information prepared for a specific purpose — such as a grant acquittal, regulatory requirement, or trust deed obligation.

The Audit Process: What to Expect

While every audit is tailored to the organisation, the general process follows these stages:

Stage 1: Initial Discussion & Planning

The auditor meets with you to understand your organisation, its structure, operations, and specific audit requirements. They assess risks, plan the audit approach, and provide a fee proposal and timeline.

Stage 2: Fieldwork & Testing

The auditor examines evidence, tests transactions, verifies balances, reviews internal controls, and makes enquiries of management. This is the most hands-on phase and may involve on-site work or remote document review.

Stage 3: Reporting

The auditor prepares the audit report with their opinion, along with a management letter containing findings and recommendations. They walk you through the results before finalising.

Who Can Perform an Audit?

In New Zealand, many statutory audits must be performed by a recognised qualified auditor. CA ANZ (Chartered Accountants Australia and New Zealand) is one recognised pathway, and other recognised bodies — such as CPA Australia — may also apply. At SA Audit Limited, our Director Sheed Ali is a Qualified Auditor recognised through CA ANZ / NZICA with over 20 years of experience.

How Long Does an Audit Take?

The timeframe depends on the size and complexity of your organisation and the condition of your records. A straightforward audit may take 2–4 weeks from fieldwork to final report, while more complex engagements can take longer. The key to an efficient audit is good preparation — well-organised records and responsive communication significantly reduce the time required.

How Much Does an Audit Cost?

Audit fees depend on the size and complexity of your organisation, the type of engagement, and the condition of your financial records. A reputable auditor will always provide a transparent, upfront fee proposal before any work begins — so there are no surprises. At SA Audit, we provide fixed-fee quotes tailored to your specific needs.

Key Takeaways

  • An audit is an independent examination of financial statements by a qualified auditor
  • Audits provide the highest level of assurance available
  • Organisations may need audits for statutory, lender, funder, or governance reasons
  • The process involves planning, fieldwork, and reporting
  • Many statutory audits in NZ must be performed by a recognised qualified auditor — CA ANZ is one recognised pathway, and other bodies may also apply
