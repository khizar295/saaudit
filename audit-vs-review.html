Audit vs Review: Which Does Your Organisation Need?

7 min read Published 2025 Sheed Ali, CA ANZ Qualified Auditor

One of the most common questions we hear is: "Do we need an audit or a review?" The answer depends on your organisation's legal requirements, stakeholder expectations, and budget. Understanding the difference between these two engagement types is crucial for making the right choice.

The Key Difference: Assurance Level

The fundamental difference between an audit and a review is the level of assurance provided:

  • Audit — Provides high (but not absolute) assurance. The auditor gives a positive opinion that the financial statements are true and fair.
  • Review — Provides moderate assurance. The auditor states whether anything has come to their attention that causes them to believe the financial statements are not properly prepared (negative assurance).

Side-by-Side Comparison

Feature Audit Review
Assurance Level High Moderate
Procedures Detailed testing, verification, controls testing Analytical procedures & enquiries
Conclusion Type Positive opinion Negative assurance
Cost Higher Lower (typically 50–70% of audit cost)
Time Required Longer Shorter
Standard ISAs (NZ) ISRE 2400 (NZ)
Disruption More Less

What Happens in an Audit?

In a financial audit, the auditor performs extensive procedures including:

  • Detailed testing of transactions and balances
  • Verification of assets and liabilities
  • Review and testing of internal controls
  • Confirmations with third parties (banks, debtors, creditors)
  • Physical verification of assets where applicable
  • Assessment of accounting policies and estimates

The result is an audit report with a positive opinion — "In our opinion, the financial statements give a true and fair view..."

What Happens in a Review?

In a review engagement, the auditor performs fewer procedures:

  • Analytical procedures (comparing current year figures with prior years, budgets, and expectations)
  • Enquiries of management and staff
  • Review of financial statements for obvious misstatements
  • Assessment of whether accounting policies are appropriate

The result is a review report with negative assurance — "Based on our review, nothing has come to our attention that causes us to believe the financial statements are not properly prepared..."

When to Choose an Audit

You should choose an audit when:

  • Your organisation is legally required to have one (large companies, charities above thresholds)
  • Your constitution or trust deed mandates an audit
  • Your lender or funder requires audited financials
  • Stakeholders need the highest level of assurance
  • You want to strengthen internal controls and governance

When to Choose a Review

A review may be appropriate when:

  • A full audit is not legally required
  • Your constitution allows a review instead of an audit
  • You need moderate assurance at a lower cost
  • Stakeholders are satisfied with moderate assurance
  • You're a smaller organisation wanting to demonstrate credibility

Can You Upgrade from a Review to an Audit?

Yes. Many organisations start with a review and move to a full audit as they grow or as requirements change. If you're unsure which is right for you, we can discuss your specific situation and advise on the most appropriate engagement type. A review can also be a stepping stone — providing some assurance now while you prepare for full audits in the future.

What About Agreed-Upon Procedures?

There's a third option worth mentioning. Agreed-upon procedures (AUP) involve performing specific procedures agreed with you and reporting factual findings — without providing any assurance opinion. AUPs are the most targeted and often the most cost-effective option when you only need verification of specific items. Learn more about AUPs.

Which Is Right for You?

The decision between an audit and a review depends on your specific circumstances. If you're unsure, contact us — we'll help you understand your obligations and recommend the most appropriate and cost-effective engagement type for your organisation.

Key Takeaways

  • Audits provide high assurance; reviews provide moderate assurance
  • Audits involve detailed testing; reviews use analytical procedures and enquiries
  • Reviews typically cost 50–70% of an audit
  • Your legal requirements and constitution determine which you need
  • You can upgrade from a review to an audit as your needs change
