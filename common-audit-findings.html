Common Audit Findings & How to Avoid Them

7 min read Published 2025 Sheed Ali, CA ANZ Qualified Auditor

After 20+ years of auditing organisations across New Zealand, we see the same issues come up time and time again. The good news? Most common audit findings are entirely preventable with good habits and simple processes. In this article, we share the most frequent findings we encounter — and practical steps you can take to avoid them.

1. Unreconciled Bank Accounts

The issue: Bank balances in the financial statements don't match the actual bank statements at year-end. This is the most common finding we see, particularly in smaller organisations.

How to avoid it:

  • Complete bank reconciliations monthly — don't leave them until year-end
  • Investigate and resolve any unreconciled differences immediately
  • Ensure all transactions are recorded in the accounting system
  • Reconcile all accounts — not just the main operating account

2. Missing or Inadequate Documentation

The issue: Transactions are recorded but there's no supporting documentation — no invoices, no receipts, no approval evidence.

How to avoid it:

  • Require supporting documents for all transactions above a set threshold
  • Implement a simple approval process — even just an email approval works
  • Store documents systematically (cloud storage or filing system)
  • Keep contracts, lease agreements, and loan documentation in a central register

3. Related Party Transactions Not Disclosed

The issue: Transactions with related parties (trustees, board members, owners, family members) are not properly identified or disclosed in the financial statements.

How to avoid it:

  • Maintain a related parties register
  • Document the nature and amount of all related party transactions
  • Ensure related party transactions are conducted on arm's length terms
  • Disclose all related party transactions in the notes to the financial statements

4. Fixed Asset Register Not Maintained

The issue: The fixed asset register is out of date — assets have been purchased or disposed of but the register hasn't been updated. Depreciation calculations may also be incorrect.

How to avoid it:

  • Update the asset register whenever assets are purchased or disposed of
  • Conduct an annual physical asset count
  • Review depreciation rates and methods annually
  • Remove fully depreciated assets that have been disposed of

5. Revenue Recognition Issues

The issue: Revenue is recognised in the wrong period — either提前 or deferred — or grant income is not properly accounted for.

How to avoid it:

  • Understand the revenue recognition criteria for your income streams
  • For grants, recognise income when conditions are met, not when received
  • Accrue revenue earned but not yet invoiced at year-end
  • Defer income received in advance for goods/services not yet delivered

6. Inadequate Segregation of Duties

The issue: One person handles multiple financial functions — e.g., receiving payments, banking, recording, and reconciling — creating a risk of fraud or error going undetected.

How to avoid it:

  • Separate key duties where possible (even in small organisations)
  • Have someone independent review bank reconciliations
  • Require dual authorisation for payments above a set threshold
  • For very small organisations, consider having a board member review transactions

7. Performance Report Issues (Charities & NFPs)

The issue: For charities and NFPs, the performance report (statement of service performance) is incomplete, vague, or doesn't align with the financial statements.

How to avoid it:

  • Start drafting the performance report early — don't leave it to the last minute
  • Be specific about what was achieved — use measurable outcomes
  • Ensure the performance report aligns with your strategic plan
  • Cross-reference performance report quantities with financial statement amounts

8. Late or Incomplete Preparation

The issue: Records aren't ready when the auditor arrives, causing delays, additional costs, and frustration on all sides.

How to avoid it:

  • Start preparing 4–6 weeks before the audit fieldwork
  • Use our audit preparation checklist
  • Communicate with your auditor about what's needed and when
  • Keep records organised throughout the year — not just at audit time

The Management Letter

After an audit, your auditor will typically provide a management letter — a document summarising findings, recommendations, and areas for improvement. This is one of the most valuable outputs of the audit process. Don't file it away — use it as a roadmap for improving your financial management and internal controls over the coming year.

Key Takeaways

  • Most audit findings are preventable with good processes
  • Monthly bank reconciliations are non-negotiable
  • Maintain supporting documentation for all transactions
  • Identify and disclose related party transactions
  • Keep your fixed asset register up to date
  • Implement segregation of duties wherever possible
  • Use the management letter as a tool for continuous improvement
